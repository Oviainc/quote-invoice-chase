Collection rate: the share of invoiced money that has actually arrived — payments received divided by everything invoiced. The weekly review watches it rise; a rate that stalls while the aging buckets grow is the signal to chase harder, oldest first.

Days to pay: the average number of days between an invoice being created and being paid in full — the days-sales-outstanding reading of the invoices on record here. It counts only invoices with a payment date on record, so it never guesses. Watch it fall as reminders land earlier; a number that climbs while the collection rate stalls is the signal to shorten terms or chase sooner.

Promise record by client

No promises yet.

Every promise a client gives is kept on record with its date — and then resolved from what actually happened: kept when the invoice was paid in full by the promised date, broken when that date passed without the payment, and still open while the date is ahead, so an open promise never counts in the rate. A promise moved before its date is replaced, never counted as broken; a promise moved after it passed stays broken on the record. The kept rate — kept of the promises that have resolved, per client — is the one number that measures whether a client's word predicts their payment: a client whose promises keep breaking is the one to ask for a deposit or a shorter promise next time.

Disputes by reason

No disputes yet.

Every dispute in one place, by the reason the client gave: how many were raised, how many are still open, and how long the resolved ones took — counted from the disputes logged on each invoice. The biggest group tells us which fix comes first: a price pattern points back at the quote, a missing-document pattern points at how invoices are sent, and a large still-open count points at answering faster. A disputed invoice is not a late one, and chasing it harder never releases the cash.

Quote outcomes

No quotes yet.

Every quote in one place, by what actually happened to it — accepted, declined, expired, or still open. Two rates count differently on purpose: the decided rate counts only the quotes a client actually decided, so silence never flatters it; the all-quote rate counts every quote sent, so the quotes that went quiet stay in the picture. Each outcome is stamped with its date when it is recorded, and the average time to an acceptance is read from those dates — an answer that arrives in days is a different signal from one that arrives after weeks of follow-up.

Follow-up composer

Pick a saved document, choose a step, and copy a ready-to-send message in the document language. Quotes get a follow-up rhythm of their own, because most quotes are never refused — they are simply never answered: a check-in a few days after sending, to confirm the quote arrived and offer to answer questions; a follow-up about a week later that adds something useful; and a closing note near the valid-until date, after which the quote comes off the list and the door stays open. The date in the Due / valid until field is the quote's valid-until date: it prints on the quote, a quote past it shows as expired, and the closing note points at that real date — never an invented deadline. Every follow-up we compose is logged on the quote, the documents list suggests the next step for every open quote — worked out from the day it was sent, the follow-ups already logged, and its valid-until date — and picking a quote here pre-selects that step. When the client decides, record the outcome on the quote — accepted, declined, or expired — so the record shows what actually happened to every quote, not only the ones that were chased as invoices: each outcome is stamped with its date, and the Quote outcomes view rolls them all up — accepted, declined, expired, and still open, with a decided conversion rate that counts only the quotes a client actually decided and an all-quote rate that counts every quote sent, plus the average time to an acceptance read from the outcome dates. When a quote is declined, we record the reason the client gave — price or budget, timing, another provider chosen, scope not a fit, or the project cancelled — or that no reason was given, and the outcomes view counts declined quotes by that reason: the pattern behind lost quotes then reads from what clients actually said, never from a price guessed out of silence. When a quote is accepted, the work is protected before it starts: record the deposit agreed on the quote — a percent of the total and the date it is due — and it prints on the quote itself, under its own label in the document language. The composer carries a deposit request written in English, French, and Spanish that states the amount and the date, says the work is scheduled as soon as the deposit arrives and the planned date is held from that moment, and states plainly that the deposit is not an extra charge — it comes off the final invoice in full. Your saved payment details travel with the request, picking an accepted quote whose deposit has not been requested yet pre-selects it, and composing it is logged on the quote like every other step. The documents list shows the deposit on every quote — due, overdue, or received — and the next step chases an overdue deposit before any work starts. Record the deposit when it arrives and it is credited on the quote at once; when the quote becomes an invoice, the deposit already received is credited on that invoice too, so the final bill asks only for the balance, never for money already paid. Heads-up, polite, firm, and final templates are written — not machine-translated word for word. A rhythm that works: a heads-up a few days before the due date, a polite note just after it, a firm note the following week, and a final note after that. Every reminder we compose is logged on the invoice, so we always know what was sent and when. The documents list suggests the next step for every unpaid invoice — worked out from its due date and the reminders already logged — and picking an invoice here pre-selects that step, so what to send next is decided before the awkward moment arrives. Part-payments count too: record a payment on any invoice in the documents list and the balance due drops at once — the dashboard, the next-step suggestion, and every reminder then ask only for what is still owed, never for money already received. When a client owes more than one invoice, tick the statement box and we compose one message per client, not one per invoice: every unpaid invoice for that client, with its due date and balance, listed in a single statement with one total — and the reminder is logged on each invoice it covers. When it reads right, send it straight from here by email or WhatsApp, or copy it into any other channel. Every reminder and statement carries your saved payment details when you have added them, so the client never has to ask how to pay — the way to pay sits in the same message that asks for it. When a client gives a date, record it as a promise on the invoice in the documents list: the list shows the promised date, the next-step suggestion waits while that date is still ahead, and once a promised date passes unpaid the suggestion returns to chasing — a promise is tracked, not just heard. Every promise stays on record and is resolved from what actually happened — kept when the invoice was paid in full by the promised date, broken when the date passed without it, still open while the date is ahead — and the Promise record by client view counts each client's kept rate from the promises that have resolved, so whether a client's word predicts their payment reads from the record too. When a client agrees a date on a call, confirm it in writing the same day: the composer carries a promise confirmation in all three languages that restates the balance and the date agreed, picking an invoice whose promise is still unconfirmed pre-selects it, and the confirmation is logged on the invoice like every other step — the agreement the promise record later resolves against is then on record from both sides, not remembered from a call. When late-payment terms are saved for the business, they print on every document and travel with every reminder and statement too, under a label in the document language — so the terms are stated where the client reads them, not remembered from an earlier conversation. When a client cannot pay in full, offer a payment plan instead of another reminder: the composer carries a plan offer in all three languages, and recording the agreed plan on the invoice — how many payments, and from which date, spaced a month apart — makes the next step wait while the plan is on track and brings it straight back to chasing the day an instalment is missed. When a client disputes an invoice, record it instead of chasing it: the dispute — the reason the client gave (price, quantity, the quality of the work, an invoice or document not received, or another reason) and the day it was raised — sits on the invoice, the suggested next step pauses the chase for that invoice only, and a per-client statement leaves a disputed invoice out while the client's other invoices stay on schedule. The composer carries a dispute acknowledgement in all three languages that confirms in writing what is in question, says the reminders for this invoice are paused while we look into it, and notes that any part of the invoice that is not in question can still be paid; picking a disputed invoice whose acknowledgement has not been logged pre-selects it, composing it is logged on the invoice like every other step, and marking the dispute resolved restarts the rhythm from where it stopped. When a payment arrives, close the loop with a thank-you: the composer carries a payment-received note in all three languages that confirms what arrived, says plainly when the invoice is settled in full, and — after a part-payment — states the balance still due without chasing it; picking a paid invoice whose thank-you has not been logged pre-selects it, and composing it is logged on the invoice like every other step.
